\section{Human Evaluation}
\label{sec:human_evaluation}
From the very beginning of machine translation, researchers have contemplated how to measure the quality of the systems they build. What makes one translation better than another? Can we find a method that allows us to evaluate MT systems in an objective and reliable way?

Human evaluation is considered the gold standard---no automatic metric can fully capture the nuances of meaning, style, or naturalness that human readers perceive. However, human evaluation comes with its own challenges---evaluations can be costly, time-consuming, and---more importantly---difficult to reproduce. Even when evaluating the same system under identical conditions, human judgments vary across annotators and can fluctuate for the same annotator over time.

Even deciding how to conduct a human evaluation is not straightforward. Over the years, various methods have been proposed to manually judge the quality of a hypothesis, ranging from assessing how well a translation preserves meaning or reads naturally in the target language to evaluating whether a user can perform a task based on automatically translated instructions. For example, \cite{Sinaiko} examined how the quality and readability of translated technical manuals (including early computer translations) affect technicians’ ability to perform maintenance tasks.

Judging machine translation quality is not a simple task. In \Cref{table:wmt15:example}, we present an example from the WMT15 \citep{wmt15} Russian-to-English translation task, in which a single Russian source sentence was translated differently by all thirteen systems. This high degree of variability illustrates the difficulty of human evaluation. Consider the challenge of comparing these translations: which undesirable attribute should be considered most crucial---nonsensical phrasing, untranslated words, inaccurate translations, shifts in sentence meaning, grammatical errors, or omitted articles? The perceived severity of these attributes is  subjective, as it depends on the annotator's linguistic priorities and the intended use case for the translation.

Furthermore, after evaluating multiple complex translations and making the difficult choice in their comparison, maintaining consistent judgments over time becomes difficult, which negatively affects reproducibility. Additionally, it is worth noting that a person's assessment of quality evolves during the evaluation process. As evaluators review numerous good and bad translations, their judgments tend to shift, resulting in a low inter-annotator agreement.  

As a result, crowd workers tend to be less reliable \citep{graham_2017} as experts, and even automatic metrics based on pre-trained embeddings can outperform human crowd workers  \citep{freitag-etal-2021-experts}, which is in contrast to previous work by \cite{bentivogli-etal-2011-getting}. 

In~Sections \ref{adequacy} to \ref{sec:esa}, we discuss several manual evaluation methods used in the Workshop on Machine Translation (WMT) general translation and metric tasks that subsequently served as gold standards for the WMT Metrics shared tasks.

\subsection{Adequacy and Fluency}
\label{adequacy}

In the first year of the Workshop on Machine Translation shared tasks \citep{koehn-monz:2006:WMT}, the evaluation of machine translation quality was approached from two distinct dimensions: fluency and adequacy. This evaluation methodology is much older than the WMT shared tasks and was already employed in large-scale machine translation evaluations conducted by the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) in the early 1990s \citep{white-etal-1994-arpa}. 

Fluency captures how natural and well-formed a translation sounds in the target language, independently of the source text. It reflects whether the translation reads like authentic text written by a native speaker. As such, it can be judged by monolingual evaluators who focus on the grammatical correctness, clarity, and naturalness of the hypothesis.

In contrast, adequacy measures how well a hypothesis preserves the meaning of the source text. This dimension requires access to both the source and target sentences, and evaluators must be fluent in both languages. Adequacy assesses the extent to which the informational content of the source is conveyed accurately and completely in the translation.

In these evaluations, a judge is typically presented with five translation hypotheses for a given source sentence and asked to rate each on a five-point scale: one scale for fluency and another for adequacy. We present an example of the evaluation interface in \cref{fig:fluency_example}.

Subsequent studies, including \cite{callison-burch-etal-2007-meta} and \cite{wmt16}, reported a high correlation between adequacy and fluency scores. In other words, it is difficult to derive meaningful content from a disfluent sentence; similarly, fully understanding a sentence often requires a high degree of fluency, since even minor morphological variation can substantially modify the meaning of an entire sentence---a phenomenon particularly evident in highly inflected languages such as Czech.

Furthermore, separating adequacy and fluency introduces the complication of recombining the two scores in a meaningful way for tasks like tuning automatic metrics. This challenge motivated later attempts to unite translation quality into a~single scale \citep{denkowski-lavie-2010-choosing, przybocki-2010}.


\subsection{Relative Ranking (RR)}
\label{relative_ranking}
Between 2008 and 2016, the primary technique of human evaluation in WMT was the \textit{relative ranking} method (RR) \citep{callison-burch-etal-2007-meta}. This approach, first proposed by \citet{vilar-etal-2007-human}, presents human annotators—fluent in both the source and target languages—with five translation hypotheses for the same source sentence. Annotators are asked to rank the translations from 1 (worst) to 5 (best), where the numerical ranks do not signify an absolute measure of quality but rather a \textit{relative ordering}. Ties in rankings are allowed.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../img/relative_ranking.png}
    \caption{Annotation interface for the relative ranking evaluation method. Reprinted from Figure 3 \cite{callison-burch-etal-2007-meta}}
    \label{fig:relative_rev}
\end{figure}

Presenting five translations simultaneously enables ten pairwise comparisons per annotation, potentially providing more comparative information in a shorter amount of time. \cref{fig:relative_rev} illustrates this method. However, this setup also increases annotators' cognitive load and may result in less reliable rankings, especially when differences between translations are subtle. Consequently, some translation tasks limit the comparison to only two translations at a time to reduce complexity and annotator fatigue \citep{braslavski-etal-2013-english}.

In particular, judges are not given explicit instructions to evaluate specific aspects of the translation system, such as fluency or adequacy. Instead, they are instructed to assess overall translation \textit{quality}, leaving the precise definition of quality to their own judgment. This absence of explicit rules is well motivated: it acknowledges that annotators, as fluent speakers of the evaluated language, are capable of making a choice based on their subjective judgment without requiring detailed guidelines.

Relative ranking yields higher inter-annotator and intra-annotator agreement (where \emph{intra-annotator agreement} refers to the consistency of the same annotator’s judgments across repeated evaluations) than absolute adequacy--fluency judgments \citep{ye-etal-2007-sentence, graham_2017}. This improvement is largely attributed to variability in how different annotators interpret numerical scales (e.g., what constitutes a score of `3' vs. `4'). \cite{song2025} further note that relative ranking methods tend to reduce noise and increase consistency in translation judgments.

However, \citet{popovic-2021-agree} reports that inter-annotator agreement diminishes when translations contain severe errors or when translations are highly similar, which highlights the limitations of subjective ranking in these edge cases. Furthermore, \cite{bojar-grains} demonstrate that five-fold rankings can be susceptible to bias introduced by exceptionally high- or low-quality translations.

\subsubsection{Aggregating Pairwise Rankings}
\label{metaevaluation}

However, a key limitation of relative ranking is the absence of an absolute measure of quality. Although this method can reliably distinguish better and worse translations, it does not indicate \textit{how much} better one translation is compared to another. 

Several aggregation methods have been proposed over time to infer absolute scores from comparative judgments. One of the first approaches was the \emph{win-rate} method, in which individual systems were scored based on how often their output was judged to be better than or equal to other competing systems \citep{wmt10}. This method, also known as the $\geq$ \emph{others} score \citep{bojar-grains}, is calculated as follows:

$$\frac{wins+ties}{wins+ties +losses}$$

\cite{bojar-grains} note two limitations of the $\geq$ \emph{others} score. First, because ties are counted as successes, systems that produce very similar outputs to others may receive artificially inflated scores. Second, the metric is sensitive to the particular set of pairwise comparisons: systems that are more frequently matched against stronger competitors may obtain lower scores, a phenomenon known as the \emph{luck-of-the-draw} effect.  To address this issue, they propose excluding ties entirely and computing system scores as follows:

$$\frac{wins}{wins+losses}$$

This method also known as $>$ \emph{others} score  became the official aggregation method for WMT12 \citep{wmt12}. However, \cite{lopez-2012-putting} shows that $>$ \emph{others} does not significantly outperform $ \ge $ \emph{others}, and instead recommends using a ranking derived from a \textit{Minimum Feedback Arc Set} (MFAS). In this approach, all pairwise preferences between systems are represented as directed edges in a graph (e.g., A → B if A is judged better than B). Because human judgments are often inconsistent, this graph may contain cycles such as A → B → C → A. The MFAS algorithm identifies the smallest set of edges whose removal breaks all such cycles, producing a consistent, acyclic ordering that agrees with the maximum possible number of pairwise comparisons. In other words, MFAS yields a ranking that best fits the observed human preferences while minimizing contradictions.

To mitigate the \textit{luck-of-the-draw} effect, a Monte Carlo model called \emph{Expected Wins} \citep{koehn-2012-simulating} was introduced in WMT13 \citep{wmt13}. This metric is both intuitive and statistically grounded: it estimates the probability that a given system's translation would be ranked higher than that of a randomly selected competing system for a randomly chosen sentence and judge.

Formally, for a system \( S_i \) evaluated among a set of systems \( \{S_j\} \), and given a set of pairwise rankings summarized by \( \text{win}(A, B) \) --- the number of times system \( A \) is ranked higher than system \( B \) --- the expected wins score is defined as:

$$
\text{score}(S_i) = \frac{1}{|\{S_j\}_{j \ne i}|} \sum_{j, j \ne i} \frac{\text{win}(S_i, S_j)}{\text{win}(S_i, S_j) + \text{win}(S_j, S_i)}
$$

This formulation provides a smoothed and probabilistic measure of the relative performance of a system, reducing the influence of outlier comparisons and making it better suited for system-level comparisons across large evaluation sets. Note that this score ignores the ties.

\cite{wmt14} compare Expected Wins with two alternative latent-variable ranking approaches: a Bayesian model proposed by \cite{hopkins-may-2013-models} and Microsoft’s TrueSkill \citep{sakaguchi-etal-2014-efficient}, a Bayesian ranking algorithm originally developed for online gaming \citep{NIPS2006_f44ee263}. 
All three methods produced nearly identical system rankings, with TrueSkill exhibiting slightly lower variance and greater robustness to sparse comparisons. Because of these practical advantages, TrueSkill was subsequently adopted as the official aggregation method from WMT14 to WMT16. In WMT17 \citep{wmt17}, RR was replaced by an absolute scoring method, and no score-combination method was needed.

% TrueSkill models each MT system as a latent variable representing translation quality, estimated from noisy pairwise human preferences using Bayesian inference. Each system is associated with a Gaussian distribution whose mean reflects estimated quality and whose variance captures uncertainty due to limited evidence. Compared to Expected Wins, TrueSkill explicitly models uncertainty and is more robust to sparse and imbalanced comparisons, which motivated its adoption as the official aggregation method in WMT14–WMT16.

\subsection{Direct Assessment (DA) and Scalar Quality Metric (SQM)}
\label{sec:direct_assessment}
The Direct Assessment (DA) framework \citep{graham-etal-2013-continuous, graham-etal-2014-machine, graham_2017} was introduced to address key limitations of previous approaches, particularly the lack of absolute quality scores and the high cognitive demand of ranking multiple translations simultaneously. Instead of comparing several outputs, DA requires annotators to evaluate each translation independently on a fine-grained scale from 0 to 100, typically implemented via a slider. 

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{direct_assessment.png}
    \caption{Example of a bilingual Direct Assessment (DA) setup. Annotators are shown a full translated document, randomly drawn from anonymized system outputs, and asked to evaluate the quality of each segment as well as the overall document using sliding scales. Reprinted from Figure 6 in \cite{barrault-etal-2020-findings}.}
    \label{fig:direct_assessment}
\end{figure}

In the DA framework, annotators assess how well a system translation expresses the meaning of a reference text. Depending on the setup, DA can be performed in two main configurations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bilingual DA:} Annotators are shown the \textit{source sentence} and the \textit{system translation}. They judge how accurately and completely the system translation conveys the meaning of the source. \Cref{fig:direct_assessment} illustrates this interface. 
    \item \textbf{Monolingual DA:} Annotators see only the \textit{reference translation} and the \textit{system translation}. The reference serves as a proxy for the source meaning, so this setup still targets adequacy, not mere fluency. It allows non-bilingual annotators to participate without loss of validity. 
\end{itemize}

In both cases, annotators assign a score between 0 and 100 to indicate the perceived adequacy. This scale, proposed by \citet{graham-etal-2013-continuous}, offers greater granularity and absolute scoring compared to relative ranking or adequacy-fluency scoring. Individual annotator scores are first \textit{z-normalized} by subtracting each annotator's mean score and dividing by their standard deviation, accounting for differences in how annotators use the rating scale. The system-level scores are then computed by averaging these normalized adequacy scores across all sentences. 

In addition, fluency scores were collected using monolingual DA without references; however, it was not included in the final score, as a translation can appear highly fluent while not conveying the source meaning \citep{wmt16}.

DA was piloted in WMT16, initially complementing official RR scores. Subsequent analysis demonstrated that DA produces higher inter-annotator agreement and stronger correlation with expert judgments than RR, especially with a high number of judges per sentence \citep{graham-etal-2017-improving}. Consequently, DA became the official WMT evaluation method in 2017. In WMT18 \citep{bojar-etal-2018-findings}, the protocol was refined: bilingual DA became the standard for translations from English into other languages (utilizing native speakers of the target language), while the monolingual setting was retained for into-English evaluation.

A crucial feature of DA is its use of \emph{quality control} items to ensure annotation reliability. Annotators are presented with a mix of high-quality reference translations and artificially degraded outputs (e.g., by removing words or other perturbations). Reliable annotators are expected to consistently assign lower scores to degraded translations than to their original counterparts. Annotators whose scores do not correlate with these known quality levels are filtered out, ensuring high data reliability even when using crowdsourcing \citep{graham_2017}.

Despite its strengths, DA is not without drawbacks. It relies on a subjective interpretation of the 0–100 scale, and because annotators focus on adequacy without explicit reference to grammar, translations that are semantically accurate but disfluent may still receive high scores. To address this, hybrid methods have been proposed to combine DA-style scalar judgment with separate fluency assessments \citep{freitag-etal-2021-experts}. Scalar Quality Metric (SQM) \citep{kocmi-etal-2022-findings} augments the DA slider with seven descriptive tick marks (e.g., ``perfect translation'', ``minor errors'', ``major errors''), providing annotators with clearer qualitative anchors. 

Since WMT22 \citep{kocmi-etal-2022-findings}, this DA+SQM configuration has been adopted as the official human evaluation protocol for the general task. It is widely regarded as the current most reliable, scalable solution that correlates well with more granular frameworks, such as the Multidimensional Quality Metrics (MQM) annotations \citep{freitag-etal-2021-experts} which we introduce in the following section.

\subsection{Multidimensional Quality Metrics (MQM)}
\label{sec:mqm}
Multidimensional Quality Metrics (MQM) \citep{lommel-etal-2014-using} is a comprehensive and systematic framework for translation quality assessment. It was originally developed in response to the professional translation industry’s need for a standardized and reliable approach to quality assurance \citep{burchardt2013multidimensional}.

In contrast to earlier evaluation methods that primarily focused on overall linguistic accuracy or grammatical correctness, MQM evaluates translation quality across multiple dimensions.  It uses a hierarchical taxonomy of error categories, requiring evaluators to identify specific types of errors — such as mistranslations, omissions, additions, word-order problems, or grammatical mistakes — and to assign each error a severity level (Minor, Major, or Critical). 
A standardized subset of this taxonomy is shown in \Cref{fig:mqm}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{MQM.png}
    \caption{MQM hierarchy of errors. Reprinted from Figure 1 in \cite{lommel-etal-2014-using}}
    \label{fig:mqm}
\end{figure}

This fine-grained approach enables a more transparent and interpretable analysis of translation quality compared to simple numeric scores. Rather than summarizing performance into one overall value, MQM makes it possible to examine which types of errors a system produces and how serious they are. \cite{freitag-etal-2021-experts} demonstrate that rankings derived from expert MQM annotations correlate more strongly with professional human judgments than DA or RR. Moreover, MQM often exposes system-specific weaknesses — for example, consistent terminology errors or frequent meaning distortions — that may remain hidden when evaluation results are condensed into aggregated overall scores that average across different error types and severity levels.

Although MQM has proven to be highly effective and is widely used for quality assurance in the translation industry, this level of detail comes at a cost. The annotation process is considerably more time-consuming and expensive than other evaluation methods, requiring trained experts capable of applying the error taxonomy consistently. This limits scalability, especially in large-scale, multilingual settings like WMT. Aggregating structured MQM annotations into system-level scores also introduces additional complexity.

Since WMT 2021, MQM-style evaluation has been adopted as the primary human evaluation method in the WMT Metrics shared task. Because of annotation costs, only a small set of language pairs (typically three) are annotated each year.

\subsection{Error Span Annotation (ESA)}
\label{sec:esa}
More recently, WMT24~\citep{kocmi-etal-2024-findings} introduced the \textit{Error Span Annotation} (ESA) framework~\citep{kocmi-etal-2024-error}.
ESA represents a hybrid inspired by DA and MQM:  DA is simple and fast, but less informative and may be less reliable, while MQM is very informative and reliable, but expensive and time-consuming. ESA combines them both. Annotators first mark error spans with just \textit{major} or \textit{minor} severity, then assign a scalar overall quality score. An example of the interface for ESA evaluation is presented in \Cref{fig:esa}.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ESA.png}
    \caption{Two screenshots illustrating the ESA interface and the corresponding annotator instructions. ESA displays multiple segments from a document simultaneously, along with any associated video sources. After highlighting the individual error spans, the annotator assigns a final segment-level score on a 0–100 scale. Reprinted from \cite{kocmi-etal-2024-findings}.}
    \label{fig:esa}
\end{figure}

In the official WMT24 evaluation setup, only the scores are used to compute the final system scores, following the standard z-normalization and averaging procedure used for DA+SQM. The error spans serve as a preliminary step that encourages annotators to identify specific issues before assigning an overall score, which improves consistency and provides diagnostic information, but they are not numerically aggregated into the final metric. \cite{kocmi-etal-2024-error} proposes optional MQM-like span-weighted scoring formulas, but they are not used for systems ranking in WMT tasks.

The authors claim ESA reduces fluency bias compared to DA+SQM, though direct human parity with MQM remains untested on shared data. \cite{freitag-etal-2024-llms} confirm that top automatic metrics yield near-identical system-level rankings for MQM and ESA.

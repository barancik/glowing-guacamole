If you hadn't nailed 'im to the perch 'e'd be pushing up the daisies! 'Is metabolic processes are now 'istory! 'E's off the twig! The paragraph above is an iconic scene from Monty Python's Dead Parrot sketch, which is arguably one of the most famous examples of paraphrasing in popular culture. It vividly illustrates the extensive variety found within natural languages, showcasing the numerous ways in which a simple fact of a bird's demise can be expressed. It highlights the richness and diversity inherent in language usage.

In fact, according to Wikipedia \citep{wiki:death}, there are more than a hundred expressions related to death, including idioms, euphemisms, and slang. Although humans may appreciate and enjoy the linguistic diversity in various contexts such as poetry, humor, or everyday speech, this diversity makes interpretation and comprehension of the intended meaning more difficult for automated systems.  Despite substantial progress in recent years, even advanced models struggle to capture semantic equivalence when paraphrases involve structural reordering, idiomatic usage, or culturally dependent formulations \citep{michail-etal-2025-paraphrasus, zhou2024paraphraseidentificationdeeplearning}. For natural language processing (NLP) applications, the ability to recognize that diverse expressions have the same underlying concept remains a persistent challenge. 

We illustrate this issue with two NLP tasks: natural language inference (NLI) and machine translation (MT) evaluation. In NLI, models assess relationships between two texts, a \emph{premise} and a \emph{hypothesis}. The task is to determine whether the meaning of the hypothesis can be inferred from the premise based on their semantic similarity. The relationship is typically categorized as \emph{entailment}, \emph{contradiction}, or \emph{neutral}, depending on whether the hypothesis logically follows from, contradicts, or is independent of the premise \citep{dagan:2005}.

A major difficulty in NLI is the variability of linguistic expression: two sentences may convey the same meaning using entirely different lexical or syntactic structures (see \Cref{nli_examples}). Lexical overlap–based methods or surface-level similarity metrics often fail to recognize such paraphrastic relationships, as they rely primarily on word matching rather than deeper semantic understanding. Consequently, models must rely on contextual representations capable of capturing meaning beyond exact wording in order to correctly identify entailment and semantic equivalence. However, even modern embedding-based models may struggle in such cases, particularly when paraphrases involve subtle semantic distinctions or complex structural variation, as we show in further detail in \Cref{costra:results}.

Similarly, automatic machine translation evaluation is challenging because a single source sentence can have many valid translations. During evaluation, a \textit{hypothesis} (the system-generated translation) is typically compared to a \textit{reference} translation produced by a human expert. However, there may be hundreds of thousands of correct ways to translate the same sentence \citep{bojar-scratching}, and many commonly used evaluation metrics, such as \bleu \citep{papineni-etal-2002-bleu}, fail to account for synonymous expressions or variations in word order. As a result, even a perfectly adequate and fluent translation may receive a low score, reflecting the metric’s reliance on surface-level string matching rather than semantic equivalence.

\Cref{example_of_BLEU_malfunction} illustrates this issue, showing how a correct and semantically accurate translation can nevertheless receive a lower score due to the lexical difference from the reference. This problem is particularly pronounced in morphologically rich languages with relatively free word order, such as Czech \citep{bojar-tackling-sparse-data}. Consequently, the ability to handle paraphrasing and meaning variation becomes crucial for a reliable MT evaluation. In particular, the role of paraphrasing within MT evaluation constitutes the central motivation of this thesis.

This thesis was originally proposed more than a decade ago, when the state-of-the-art in NLP was considerably different from that today. At that time, paraphrasing was considered a key strategy for addressing the variability of correct translations in  machine translation evaluation. Metrics such as \mn \citep{denkowski-lavie-2014-meteor} or \terp \citep{terp}  incorporated paraphrase tables and synonym matching to reduce the dependence on the exact word overlap. Another approach, to make string-based metrics more reliable, was the automatic paraphrasing of reference sentences to cover a wider range of acceptable translation variants. The first part of this thesis revisits this line of research, exploring ways to improve string-based evaluation through the use of paraphrasing resources and targeted reference expansion for Czech.

Over the past decade, however, the field of machine translation evaluation has changed dramatically. Neural embedding-based metrics and large-language-model-based metrics (e.g., \bleurt \citep{sellam2020bleurt}, \comet \citep{rei-etal-2020-comet}, \textsc{GEMBA} \citep{kocmi-federmann-2023-gemba}) now achieve significantly higher correlation with human judgments, which largely reduces the need for explicit paraphrasing.

At the same time, embedding-based approaches represents a fundamentally different way of handling paraphrastic variation. Instead of enumerating discrete paraphrases or synonym lists, embeddings represent sentences as points in a continuous vector space, where semantically similar formulations are mapped to nearby regions. In this sense, embeddings implicitly capture a potentially unbounded set of alternative expressions, providing a more flexible and scalable representation of meaning than explicit paraphrase collections.

Nevertheless, despite the clear advantages of modern evaluation metrics, string-based metrics remain widely used in research and shared tasks. In a large study, \cite{marie-etal-2021-scientific} showed that between 2010 and 2021 more than 100 new evaluation metrics were proposed, yet approximately 99\% of MT studies still relied on \bleu for system comparison. Among these newer metrics, only \texttt{RIBES} \citep{ribes:isozaki-etal-2010-automatic} and \chrf \citep{popovic-2015-chrf} have gained modest adoption, while \bleu, \ter \citep{ter} and \mn \citep{denkowski-lavie-2014-meteor} remained the most widely used ones. 

This situation has changed substantially since \citeyear{marie-etal-2021-scientific}. It has become more common to publish results using modern metrics such as \bleurt \citep{sellam-etal-2020-bleurt} and \comet \citep{rei-etal-2020-comet, rei-etal-2022-cometkiwi}, each of which has accumulated more than a thousand citations. There is also strong pressure to report additional metrics alongside \bleu, as recent analyses \citep{mathur-etal-2020-tangled, freitag-etal-2022-results} confirm that \bleu and other surface-level metrics often correlate poorly with human evaluation and urge the community to go beyond them.

However, \bleu remains widely used for several practical reasons: its simplicity and interpretability, comparability with previous work and the lack of transparency and trust in “black-box” neural evaluation models \citep{leiter2023explainable}. Furthermore, some reviewers  continue to require \bleu scores for publication, further reinforcing its continued use \citep{marie-etal-2021-scientific}.

This thesis reflects both perspectives --- the traditional and the contemporary. The earlier chapters revisit paraphrasing as a once-key strategy for addressing the limitations of surface-based metrics. Although embedding-based methods reduce the need for explicit paraphrasing, paraphrasing remains a useful complementary strategy, particularly in settings where transparency, interpretability, or compatibility with traditional metrics is required.

The later chapters shift toward the evaluation of sentence embeddings, as embedding-based metrics currently represent the state-of-the-art in MT evaluation. We introduce a dataset for thorough testing of Czech sentence embeddings and investigate which models provide the strongest foundation for evaluating Czech translations. Furthermore, we examine whether a high-quality embedding for translation evaluation can be identified solely on the basis of its ability to capture meaning and sentence-level similarity.

Taken together, this thesis follows the field’s transition from paraphrase-driven fixes for string-based evaluation to embedding-based approaches that more directly model semantic similarity---while also acknowledging the continued practical and historical reliance on traditional metrics.

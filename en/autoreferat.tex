\chapwithtoc{Introduction}

During the last decade, machine translation (MT) systems have changed significantly
progressing from phrase-based statistical systems to neural models and, more recently, 
to large language models. 
Although translation quality has improved dramatically, reliable evaluation of translation 
outputs remains challenging. Reliable evaluation is still very important: 
it is needed to compare systems, guide model development, interpret shared-task results, 
and make research claims comparable across time.

Human evaluation remains the gold standard for assessing translation quality. However,
it is expensive, time-consuming, subjective, and difficult to reproduce at scale.
Consequently, automatic metrics are important component of MT research. 

The main challenge for machine translation is that a single source sentence can be 
translated correctly in many different ways. A useful metric must decide whether a 
hypothesis and a reference translation, i.e. sentence translated by professional human
translator, express the same meaning even when they differ lexically, morphologically, 
or syntactically.

Rich morphology and relatively free word order of the Czech language allow many surface 
variants that preserve the same meaning. Traditional string-based metrics such as \bleu 
can penalize correct translations simply because they differ from the reference wording 
(see \Cref{example_of_BLEU_malfunction}). Modern neural metrics reduce this limitation 
by relying on learned semantic representations, but they introduce new questions: what 
do sentence embeddings actually encode, can they reliably encode the meaning of sentence?

This dissertation follows both sides of this development. The first part revisits the 
traditional setting and asks whether string-based evaluation can be improved by 
automatically paraphrasing reference translations. The second part studies sentence 
embeddings as a modern semantic alternative, examining their geometric structure, their 
behavior on Czech semantic transformations, and their performance inside neural MT 
evaluation metrics.

Although the underlying technologies differ substantially, both parts address the same 
research question:  how automatic evaluation can better capture semantic equivalence 
between translations. More specifically, the thesis addresses four research questions: 

\begin{itemize}
\item Can the reliability of traditional string based methods be improved through 
      targeted paraphrasing of reference sentences?
\item Do sentence embeddings exhibit systematic geometric structure corresponding to semantic transformations?
\item How well do modern sentence embedding models capture complex semantic transformations in Czech?
\item Does strong perfomance on intrinsic semantic benchmarks imply strong performance as
       a machine translation evaluation metric?
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Machine Translation Evaluation}

The thesis first establishes the evaluation setting in which the experiments are interpreted. 
We present a detailed survey of machine translation evaluation methods from the last twenty 
years at the Workshop on Machine Translation (WMT) shared tasks from adequacy and fluency 
judgments through relative ranking to Direct Assessment, MQM, and Error Span Annotation.
These protocols differ in cost, granularity, and reproducibility, but they share the same 
goal: to estimate how much a translation preserves the meaning of the source. They are also 
important because their scores serve as training or validation targets for many automatic metrics. 

Automatic evaluation metrics attempt to approximate human judgments efficiently and reproducibly. 
Traditional metrics such as \bleu \citep{papineni-etal-2002-bleu}, \ter, and character-level 
metrics measure surface overlap between a system hypothesis and one or more human references. 
Their advantages are practical: they are fast, deterministic, transparent, and comparable with 
a large body of previous work. Their weakness is conceptual: lexical overlap is only an imperfect 
proxy for translation quality. Correct translations may use different words, inflectional forms, 
or word order than the reference.

This limitation motivated metrics that use linguistic resources. Metrics such as \meteor 
and \mn add stemming, synonym matching, and paraphrase tables, making the comparison more 
tolerant of legitimate variation. However, the added resources also introduce noise and 
language dependence. In the Czech setting, automatically created paraphrase tables contain 
many pairs that are not valid paraphrases in context. When these pairs are used directly 
inside a metric, the metric can reward grammatical errors, inappropriate substitutions, or 
partially untranslated output.

Recent neural metrics, including \bleurt \citep{sellam2020bleurt} and \comet 
\citep{rei-etal-2020-comet}, shift the problem from explicit string matching to learned 
semantic representations. They often correlate better with human judgments, but they are 
less transparent and depend on training data, model architecture, and evaluation protocol. 
At the same time, traditional metrics remain widely reported in MT research 
\citep{marie-etal-2021-scientific}. For this reason, the thesis studies both paradigms: 
explicit paraphrasing for string-based metrics and sentence embeddings for neural semantic 
evaluation.

\chapter{Targeted Sentence Paraphrasing for MT Evaluation}

The first experimental part investigates targeted paraphrasing of reference translations. 
The motivation is simple: if a metric penalizes a valid hypothesis because it is lexically 
different from the reference, the evaluation can be improved by generating additional 
reference variants that are closer to the hypothesis while preserving the original meaning. 
This approach keeps the metric itself mostly unchanged and instead improves the material 
against which the hypothesis is compared.

\paragraph{Simple substitution.}
The first method is deliberately conservative. It identifies candidate paraphrase pairs 
between a hypothesis and its reference, validates them against lexical and phrasal paraphrase 
resources, substitutes selected expressions into the reference, and optionally applies Depfix 
to repair grammatical errors introduced by the substitution. The method is inspired by earlier 
work on paraphrasing for automatic evaluation \citep{kauchak}, but it is adapted to Czech
morphology and to the limited reliability of Czech paraphrase resources.

This work demonstrated that evaluation quality can be improved without modifying \bleu 
itself, simply by enriching the reference space. The strongest improvements come from 
one-word substitutions selected from higher-quality resources and followed by grammatical 
correction. Multi-word paraphrases increase coverage, but they are more vulnerable to 
noise. The main lesson is that paraphrase precision and grammatical well-formedness 
matter more than the complexity of the generation method.

\paragraph{\parmesan.}
The next experiment asks whether paraphrased references are still useful for metrics 
that already include paraphrase matching internally, especially \mn. Several variants 
of \mn are compared with progressively filtered paraphrase tables and with three reference 
types: original references, paraphrased references, and Depfix-corrected paraphrased 
references. The best configuration removes internal paraphrase matching from \mn and 
evaluates Depfix-corrected paraphrased references using exact matching.

This leads to the \parmesan metric, which shows that paraphrases are more effective 
as alternative references than as internal matching rules. The result is conceptually 
important: paraphrases help when they become grammatical alternative references, but
noisy paraphrase tables can harm evaluation when they are used directly as metric-internal 
matching rules. The method therefore separates two tasks that are often conflated: 
generating plausible reference variants and scoring the final hypothesis-reference match.


\paragraph{Paraphrasing via machine translation.}
The thesis also explores whether more advanced methods of paraphrasing can improve on 
the simple substitution method. Paraphrasing and translation are closely related tasks, 
they both aim to preserve meaning of a sentence while changing its surface form, however
there are much more resources and tools available for translation than for paraphrasing.
Therefore, we adapted existing machine translation systems for monolingual paraphrasing. 

First, we adapt Moses, a phrase-based statistical MT system, for monolingual Czech-to-Czech 
paraphrasing. Czech paraphrase tables are used as translation tables, and a new decoder 
feature guides generation toward paraphrases that overlap with the evaluated hypothesis. 
Further, we experiment with adapting Treex, a deep-syntactic transfer-based MT system, 
for monolingual targeted paraphrasing.

These systems are more expressive, but they do not consistently outperform the simpler 
method. Their outputs can introduce broader structural changes and additional errors, 
making the resulting references less controlled and sometimes less useful for evaluation. 
This finding is one of the main empirical results of the first part: for MT evaluation, 
a simple, precise, grammatically corrected paraphrase can be more valuable than a 
sophisticated generator.

\paragraph{Findings.}
Overall, the paraphrasing experiments show that reference expansion can improve 
traditional metrics for Czech MT evaluation, but only when paraphrases are targeted, 
precise, and grammatically sound. Larger paraphrase resources are not automatically
better because noise can outweigh coverage. The results remain relevant even in the 
era of neural metrics: string-based metrics are still widely reported, and the 
paraphrasing experiments make explicit where surface matching fails.

\chapter{Sentence Embedding Evaluation}

In the second part, we focus on sentence embeddings evaluation. Embedding-based metrics 
represent sentences as points in a continuous vector space, where geometric similarity 
is expected to correspond to semantic similarity. This decreases the need for explicit
paraphrasing, but it introduces a new question: what semantic information is actually 
encoded in sentence embeddings, and is it encoded in a way that is useful for MT evaluation?

\paragraph{Linear relations in embedding spaces.}
In the first section of the second part, we investigate whether sentence embeddings 
exhibit consistent linear structures analogous to word embeddings. As \cite{word2vec} 
demonstrated, word embeddings capture semantic and syntactic relationships geometrically, 
which they evaluated through tests like adjective declension (easy'' $\rightarrow$ easier'' 
→ easiest''), verb tense changes (walking'' → walk''), and capital-country matching (Athens'' 
→ ``Greece'').

represents a continuous multi-dimensional vector space where individual sentences are

The lower plot illustrates the corresponding ``space of operations.'' Here, a vector

The underlying hypothesis is that consistent semantic changes (e.g., all vector 
transformations extracted from sentence pairs differing in the speed of travel ``running 
instead of walking'') should be represented close to each other in the space of operations, 
i.e., form a more or less compact \emph{cluster}.}
\label{space-of-sentences}
\end{figure}

Our objective is to determine whether \emph{whole sentence} embeddings exhibit the same linear 
behavior, looking for an analogous behavior to that observed with word embeddings. This idea is
illustrated in \cref{space-of-sentences}. Specifically, we examine whether semantic shifts 
between sentences can be modeled as transformation vectors. Understanding whether sentence
embeddings exhibit consistent linear structure is particularly important for embedding-based 
evaluation metrics, which implicitly rely on geometric properties such as vector similarity 
and compositionality.

The results show that some sentence embedding models do contain coherent geometric structure. 
Certain lexical and semantic changes form recognizable clusters, indicating that the models 
encode more than arbitrary surface similarity. At the same time, this structure depends 
strongly on the training objective. Architecture alone is not sufficient: untrained or 
poorly suited models do not show the same behavior. Negation and more complex logical changes 
remain difficult across models.

\paragraph{The \costra dataset.}
The major contribution of this thesis is \costra, a Czech benchmark for evaluating 
sentence embeddings \citep{costra10,costra11}. It consists of 7 thousand 
manually created sentences and 16 thousand pairwise human similarity judgments 
for evaluation. The dataset was designed to test whether sentence embeddings 
capture complex semantic relations beyond surface similarity. The sentences
are selected to create a real challenge for the embeddings, e.g., what is closer to original
sentence - a distinct paraphrase or sentence that is similar on the surface but different in 
meaning?  Other categories include temporal changes, modality, opposition, generalization or
style changes.

Experiments on \costra show that current sentence encoders still rely heavily on surface 
similarity. Even strong models struggle when lexical overlap is high but meaning changes 
substantially, and they often fail to capture ordinal semantic progressions. Czech-specific 
and multilingual models each have strengths, but no tested model fully solves the diagnostic 
tasks. The \costra results therefore reveal limitations that are often hidden by standard 
semantic textual similarity benchmarks.

\paragraph{Intrinsic vs. extrinsic evaluation.}

In the previous section, we demonstrated through intrinsic evaluation that sentence 
embeddings often rely on surface-level similarities rather than capturing complex 
linguistic phenomena. However, the primary focus of this thesis is extrinsic 
evaluation --- machine translation evaluation (MTE). 

In contrast to the simple mathematical computations used in intrinsic evaluation, 
extrinsic evaluation for MTE requires costly model training. This leads to a natural 
question: can intrinsic evaluation serve as a predictor for downstream performance? 
Semantic Textual Similarity (STS) is closely related to MTE, as both tasks assess 
whether two sentences convey the same meaning despite surface differences. If intrinsic 
benchmarks correlated well with extrinsic performance, they could be used as a quick 
proxy for selecting promising embedding models. 

This question is particularly relevant given the growing number of available embedding 
models. Multilingual encoders such as \labse and \xlmr provide strong cross-lingual 
representations, while language-specific models aim to better capture properties of 
individual languages. For morphologically rich languages such as Czech, it is not 
obvious which approach is more suitable for MT evaluation. This raises another question: 
do Czech-specific encoders outperform multilingual models in evaluation of Czech translations?

In the final experiments, we compare intrinsic evaluation of Czech sentence embeddings 
with their downstream usefulness in MT evaluation \citep{barancikova-bojar-2025-intrinsic}. 
Several Czech and multilingual encoders are evaluated on \costra and Czech STS datasets. 
The same encoders are then used inside \comet-based metrics trained for machine translation 
evaluation and quality estimation on WMT English-to-Czech data.

The results reveal a striking mismatch. Models that perform best on intrinsic semantic 
benchmarks do not necessarily become the best MT evaluation metrics after fine-tuning. 
Conversely, some models with weak intrinsic performance become highly competitive 
inside \comet-based metrics. Task-specific fine-tuning changes the model ranking 
substantially, which challenges the assumption that intrinsic semantic quality is a 
reliable shortcut for choosing encoders for MT evaluation.

The comparison also shows that reference-free quality estimation can become competitive 
with reference-based evaluation in this setup. This agrees with recent developments in
MT evaluation, where source-aware and reference-free neural metrics often perform on par 
with reference-based metrics. The thesis therefore concludes that embeddings must be selected 
and validated with respect to the target task, not only with respect to standalone semantic 
benchmarks.

\chapwithtoc{Conclusion}

This thesis examines two ways of addressing the same fundamental problem: automatic 
metrics must evaluate meaning, but meaning is not tied to a single surface form. The 
first part uses explicit paraphrases to make traditional metrics more tolerant of valid 
translation variants. The second part analyzes sentence embeddings as implicit semantic 
representations used by modern neural metrics.

Across both parts, the same conclusion emerges: the most complex method is not necessarily 
the most effective one. Simple lexical paraphrases, when selected carefully and corrected 
grammatically, improve evaluation more reliably than more ambitious paraphrase generators. 
Similarly, larger or intrinsically stronger embedding models do not automatically produce 
better MT evaluation metrics after fine-tuning. What matters is alignment between the method, 
the data, and the specific notion of semantic equivalence required by the evaluation task.

The dissertation contributes practical resources and methodological insights: targeted 
paraphrasing methods for Czech MT evaluation, the \parmesan metric, an analysis of linear 
structure in sentence embeddings, the \costra benchmark for Czech sentence embeddings, 
and an empirical comparison of intrinsic and extrinsic embedding evaluation. Together, 
these contributions document the transition from surface-based evaluation toward semantic 
evaluation while showing that semantic evaluation remains difficult, language-sensitive, 
and task-dependent.

This thesis explores two complementary approaches to automatic machine translation 
evaluation to address the challenges of measuring semantic equivalence: targeted 
paraphrasing for traditional string-based metrics and the analysis of modern sentence 
embeddings. 

----
This thesis examines two complementary approaches to automatic machine translation evaluation. The first part focuses on targeted paraphrasing of reference translations as a method to improve the reliability of traditional string-based evaluation metrics. The second part examines the behavior and limitations of modern sentence embedding models with respect to machine translation evaluation. Together, these two approaches highlight the difficulty of measuring semantic equivalence in machine translation.

In addition to the experimental work, the thesis provides a structured overview of machine translation evaluation methods, with a particular focus on human evaluation in the WMT shared tasks. We trace the development from early adequacy and fluency judgments through relative ranking to Direct Assessment, MQM, and more recent approaches such as ESA. This overview clarifies what current evaluation protocols actually measure, as well as their practical limitations, including subjectivity, cost, and limited reproducibility. It also provides an essential context for interpreting automatic metrics, which are typically trained to approximate these human judgments.

Building upon this theoretical foundation, the main contributions of this thesis can be summarized as follows. First, we demonstrate that the reliability of string-based metrics such as \bleu can be systematically improved --- specifically in the context of a morphologically rich language like Czech --- by creating artificial reference sentences closer to machine translation outputs. We show that for Czech, simple substitution-based paraphrasing methods consistently outperform more complex approaches based on statistical or rule-based machine translation systems. This finding suggests that controlled lexical variation is more effective than generating complete paraphrases that differ more significantly in structure. At the same time, we show that large, automatically created paraphrase resources can reduce evaluation quality by introducing noise. Filtering and moving paraphrase processing from evaluation to preprocessing improves results. Finally, we establish that grammatical correctness plays a crucial role: linguistically well-formed paraphrases consistently improve evaluation reliability.

Second, we provide a detailed analysis of sentence embeddings as a semantic evaluation tool. We analyze the geometric properties of both English and multilingual sentence embedding spaces and show that certain models exhibit a clear and interpretable geometric structure, in which simple linguistic transformations correspond to consistent directional shifts. This structure is not a trivial byproduct of architecture, but emerges from the training objective, particularly in models trained with semantic or contrastive signals.

To support further examination of embedding properties, we introduce the \costra dataset of complex Czech sentence transformations, addressing a gap in existing evaluation resources. Our experiments show that even strong, state-of-the-art embedding models continue to rely heavily on surface-level similarity and often fail to capture more complex semantic relations. We also find that strong performance on intrinsic evaluation tasks does not reliably predict performance in machine translation evaluation, despite both being designed to assess semantic similarity.

Taken together, the results lead to a consistent conclusion: a more complex model does not automatically result in better evaluation performance. Simple string-based metrics, when supported by carefully constructed and grammatically sound paraphrases, can perform as well as or better than more complex approaches. Similarly, larger and more general sentence embedding models do not necessarily outperform smaller or more specialized models when applied to machine translation evaluation. These findings demonstrate that evaluation quality depends primarily on how well a method is matched to the specific task, rather than on model complexity alone.

At the same time, several open questions remain. In particular, it is unclear why intrinsic and extrinsic evaluation lead to contradictory conclusions about embedding quality. A deeper understanding of this mismatch would require a more precise characterization of what aspects of meaning are captured by human evaluation and how these relate to current intrinsic benchmarks. We leave this as future work: designing intrinsic evaluation methods that better reflect the requirements of downstream tasks such as machine translation evaluation.

Another promising direction for future work is the deeper exploration of sentence representations with interpretable geometric properties, as observed in our analysis of embedding spaces. Understanding what training conditions lead to these structured spaces---and how to mitigate their failures in areas like logical negation---may contribute to better generalization and more interpretable models.

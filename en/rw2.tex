The evolution of automatic MT evaluation can be categorized into three historical paradigms:
 (1) \textit{lexical overlap} metrics (e.g., \bleu), which dominated the statistical MT era;
 (2) \textit{neural encoder-based} metrics (e.g., \comet), which aligned with the rise of Transformers; and 
 (3) \textit{generative LLM-based} evaluators (e.g., \textsc{MetricX}), which represent the current 2026 state-of-the-art. While lexical metrics provided the foundation, the field has decisively moved toward modeling semantic adequacy through high-capacity language models.

 \subsection{Feature-Engineered Paraphrase Metrics}
Before the advent of deep contextual embeddings, "second-generation" metrics like \mn \citep{denkowski-lavie-2014-meteor} and \terp \citep{terp} attempted to bridge the gap between
string matching and semantic adequacy. These metrics utilized explicit linguistic resources 
such as WordNet synsets or Porter stemmers. While historically significant for introducing 
recall and semantic variation into evaluation, these methods are largely considered legacy 
in 2026, having been superseded by the implicit semantic modeling of neural architectures.

\subsection{LLM as Judge and Explainable Evaluation}
By 2026, the paradigm has shifted from providing a single scalar score to \textit{explainable evaluation}. Modern iterations of \textsc{MetricX-24} and agentic frameworks now provide error taxonomies (Auto-MQM) alongside scores, identifying specific mistranslations or hallucinations. While early LLM-metrics faced reproducibility issues due to proprietary API shifts, the current standard relies on distilled, open-weight "judge models" that provide stable, deterministic evaluations equivalent to human expert MQM annotations \citep{cui2025llmsscorersrethinkingmt}.

\subsection{LLM-Driven Reference Augmentation}
The traditional difficulty of obtaining multiple human references has been mitigated in recent years through LLM-based augmentation. Rather than the rule-based synonym substitution seen in earlier works \citep{kauchak}, modern approaches use high-capacity models to generate "synthetic reference bundles." These bundles represent the valid paraphrastic space of a translation, allowing metrics to distinguish between stylistic variation and semantic error more effectively than single-reference evaluation.

\section{Targeted Paraphrasing at a Deep Syntactic Layer}
\label{treex}
%\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{treex}

In this chapter,\footnote{This chapter is based on the article \citep{barancikova-rosa-2015-targeted}, which is a joint work with Rudolf Rosa. Rudolf wrote the two new Treex blocks. My responsibilities included data preparation, paraphrasing and evaluation of experiments.} we investigate targeted paraphrasing of reference sentences at the deep syntactic layer. We use the Treex framework \citep{treex} and extend it with modules for controlled paraphrasing and word-order adjustment. Our goal is to determine whether linguistically informed modifications of reference sentences improve the correlation between automatic evaluation metrics and human judgment.

In the previous section, we explored targeted paraphrasing using the SMT system Moses, adapted for monolingual phrase-based translation. The results of that approach were inconclusive, suggesting that Moses may not be well suited for targeted paraphrasing in scenarios where large, high-quality paraphrase tables are unavailable. In particular, the need to construct extensive translation tables, define targeting features, and tune complex weight configurations motivated the exploration of a more linguistically controlled, rule-based approach.

To address these limitations, we adopt a rule-based approach that enables controlled, partial sentence modifications. In particular, we adapt the Treex \citep{treex} framework, a highly modular NLP system that incorporates the TectoMT \citep{tectomt} rule-based machine translation system. TectoMT provides analysis and synthesis pipelines that transform sentences from the surface word layer to  the deep syntactic layer and back. It also supports controlled modifications at the deep syntactic level, including lexical substitutions. This makes it an ideal platform for targeted paraphrasing.

Using this pipeline, both the hypothesis and its corresponding reference sentence are analyzed to the deep syntactic layer. At this layer, only content words are represented as nodes in dependency trees, while function words are encoded through node attributes. We identify lemmas that occur in only one of the two sentences and selectively substitute lemmas from the hypothesis into the reference sentence according to paraphrase tables, producing a modified dependency tree. The modified structure is then synthesized back to the surface word layer, where appropriate word forms are generated automatically.

This targeted approach addresses several problematic issues that have previously been encountered when using Moses. By directly comparing a hypothesis and a reference, the potential for introducing noise is substantially reduced. Furthermore, Treex's NLP pipeline significantly lowers the risk of generating ungrammatical sentences. By modifying only specific parts of sentences that are directly related to a paraphrased word, while keeping the remaining structure intact, we obtain conservative paraphrases that preserve meaning and context more reliably than phrase-based methods.

\subsection{Treex}
Treex is an open-source NLP framework available on GitHub,\footurl{https://github.com/ufal/treex} including the two blocks that we contributed.\footnote{Since 2017, Treex is no longer actively developed, as several better alternatives are available (such as Udapi \citep{popel-etal-2017-udapi} or UDPipe \citep{straka-2018-udpipe}).}

Treex implements a stratificational approach to language, adopted from the Functional Generative Description theory \citep{FGP} and its later extensions within the Prague Dependency Treebank project, including PDT 2.0 \citep{pdt20}, PDT 3.0 \citep{PDT3.0}, and the consolidated annotation framework PDT-C 2.0 \citep{pdtc20}. For a more detailed description of PDT, as well as a comprehensive exploration of the various layers of the language system and their associated linguistic phenomena, see especially \cite{hajicova-tal-tecto-2000,pdt-for-ling-research-16}.

The PDT schema represents sentences at four layers: 

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{w-layer (word layer):} A sentence is represented without any linguistic annotation, consisting only of the sequence of words/tokens as they appear in the source text.
\item \textbf{m-layer (morphological layer):} At this level, each token is lemmatized to its dictionary form and assigned with a tag indicating its part of speech and other morphological categories, as relevant in the given context. The input text is also normalized (e.g., incorrect tokenization or spelling errors are corrected, numerical expressions are normalized).
\item \textbf{a-layer (analytical layer / shallow-syntax layer):} The analytical layer represents a sentence as a surface syntactic dependency tree, where nodes correspond to individual tokens and edges representing syntactic relations between them.
\item \textbf{t-layer (tectogrammatical layer / deep-syntax layer):} At this layer, a sentence is represented as a deep-syntactic dependency tree in which only content words (i.e., semantically full lexical units) have their own nodes. Each node in this tree (t-node) consists of a~t-lemma and a~set of~attributes --- most importantly for our purposes, a \textit{formeme}, indicating the original syntactic form, and \textit{grammatemes}, representing the semantic counterparts of relevant morphological features.
\end{itemize}

In our experiments with rule-based paraphrase generation, we utilize both the analysis and synthesis pipelines implemented in Treex within the TectoMT pipeline. Our paraphrasing pipeline consists of five main stages:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Analysis:} Both the hypothesis and its corresponding reference sentence are processed by the analysis pipeline, which converts them into their t-layer representations (\Cref{sect:treex:analysis}).

\item \textbf{Paraphrasing:} At the t-layer, selected t-lemmas in the reference sentence are replaced with paraphrases from the hypothesis (\Cref{sect:treex:paraphrasing}).

\item \textbf{Synthesis from t-layer to a-layer:} The modified reference tree is passed through the synthesis pipeline (\Cref{sect:treex:t2a}).

\item \textbf{Reordering:} The word order of the modified reference tree is reordered at the analytical layer so that its word order more closely matches that of the hypothesis while respecting syntactic structure (\Cref{sect:treex:reordering}).

\item \textbf{Synthesis:} Finally, the modified tree representation is synthesized back to the surface word layer, producing the final paraphrased reference sentence (\Cref{sect:treex:a2w}).
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Analysis from w-layer to t-layer}
\label{sect:treex:analysis}
The analysis from the w-layer to the a-layer within the Treex framework includes several essential NLP tasks. Initially, tokenization is performed using an internal Treex tokenizer, which segments the text into words or tokens. Following this, part-of-speech (POS) tagging and lemmatization are performed using MorphoDiTa \citep{morphodita}. Additionally, dependency parsing is performed using the MSTParser \citep{McDonald:2005} adapted by \cite{Novak:2007}, trained on the PDT corpus. It constructs the dependency relationships between tokens, forming a structured representation of the sentence's syntactic organization.


In the next step, the surface-syntactic a-tree is converted into a deep-syntactic t-tree using the standard Treex blocks as implemented in the TectoMT framework. During this transformation, auxiliary words are removed and their functions are now represented using t-node attributes (esp. \textit{formemes} and \textit{grammatemes}) of content words they belong to (e.g., two a-nodes of the analytical verb form \samppair{spal jsem}{(I) slept} are merged into a single t-node \samppair{spát}{sleep} with the tense grammateme set to the past tense. Similarly, the phrase \samppair{v květnu}{in May} is collapsed into \samppair{květen}{May} with the formeme indicating the prepositional group \samppair{v+X}{in+X}.

The choice to use the t-layer is motivated by its abstraction from surface syntax. At this level, words are represented in their lemmatized forms, which removes much of the syntactic variability present at lower layers. In addition, function words, typically not suitable targets for paraphrasing and a frequent source of noise in paraphrase tables, are not represented at the t-layer, thereby reducing the risk of generating erroneous substitutions.

This choice also aligns naturally with the resources we employ, as both \fm and \wordnet provide paraphrase pairs in lemmatized form. As a result, the t-layer makes an ideal level for applying paraphrase transformations. This abstraction simplifies the identification of paraphrasable elements, enhancing the quality and relevance of the generated paraphrases.



\subsection{Paraphrasing at t-layer}
\label{sect:treex:paraphrasing}

The paraphrasing module T2T::ParaphraseSimple\footurl{https://github.com/ufal/treex/blob/master/lib/Treex/Block/T2T/ParaphraseSimple.pm} is developed by Rudolf Rosa. It performs selective replacement of t-lemmata of t-nodes in a reference sentence.
Candidate paraphrases are obtained from \wordnet and the \fm paraphrase tables. Specific constraints ensure that the substitution remains coherent with both the hypothesis and the reference sentence.

For each lemma $A$ in the reference sentence and each lemma $B$ in the hypothesis, we consider replacing $A$ with $B$. The substitution is performed only if $A$ and $B$ are listed as paraphrases and if the replacement increases lexical overlap with the hypothesis (i.e., $A$ does not occur in the hypothesis and $B$ does not occur in the reference). 

All other attributes of the t-node are preserved, reflecting the assumption that deep syntactic properties are largely independent of the specific lexical realization. However, in practice, there is at least one case where this is not true: t-nodes corresponding to nouns are marked for grammatical gender, which is very often a grammatical property of the given lemma with no effect on the meaning, esp. for inanimate nouns (for example, the English noun \trans{house} could correspond to the Czech masculine noun \foreign{dům} or the feminine noun \foreign{budova}, both of which carry different grammatical genders but denote the same set of objects). 

To resolve this issue, the module deletes the value of the corresponding gender grammateme of the noun's t-node when paraphrasing the noun. This action prevents any incorrect gender assumptions from being carried over to the new lemma. The subsequent synthesis pipeline within Treex then generates the correct value of the morphological gender feature. This feature is critical for ensuring correct agreement in the new reference (whether it involves subject-verb agreement, agreement of the noun's attributes or, in rare cases, agreement with its complement).

An example of the method is presented in \Cref{fig:example2}.

\subsection{Synthesis from t-layer to a-layer}
\label{sect:treex:t2a}
This phase is performed by the standard TectoMT pipeline. In this phase, a-nodes corresponding to auxiliary words and punctuation are generated, morphological feature values on a-nodes are initialized and set to enforce morphological agreement among the nodes.  Correct inflectional forms are generated using MorphoDiTa based on lemma, POS, and morphological features.

\subsection{Tree-based reordering at the a-layer}
\label{sect:treex:reordering}

During synthesis, the paraphrased reference sentence is reordered at the analytical layer. The goal is to make the word order of the reference more similar to that of the hypothesis, while preserving the dependency structure. Intuitively, the procedure projects the word order of the hypothesis onto the reference, moving only entire subtrees.

At a high level, the algorithm assigns each node (or subtree) a preferred position based on its counterpart in the hypothesis and then locally reorders siblings to match these preferences. When no reliable alignment is available, nodes inherit the position of their neighbors, which naturally preserves the original word order.

The reordering is implemented in the Treex block \texttt{A2A::ReorderByLemmas} \footurl{https://github.com/ufal/treex/blob/master/lib/Treex/Block/A2A/ReorderByLemmas.pm} developed by Rudolf Rosa. The procedure consists of the following steps, illustrated in \Cref{reordering}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Alignment:}
    Lemmas in the paraphrased reference tree (reference in the rest of this section) that also occur in the hypothesis are aligned. Only lemmas that occur exactly once in both the reference and the hypothesis are aligned; all others remain unaligned.
    % Words that do not appear in the hypothesis are left unaligned, because reordering them would have little effect on word-based MT evaluation metrics. For simplicity, repeated words are also considered unaligned, since resolving ambiguity among multiple possible alignments is non-trivial. In practice, such repetitions are relatively rare.

    \item \textbf{Node MT order:}
    Each aligned node $v$ in the reference is assigned a \emph{node MT order}, $\text{NodeMT}(v)$, corresponding to the position of its counterpart in the hypothesis. Unaligned nodes have undefined $\text{NodeMT}$.

    \item \textbf{Subtree MT order:}
    The reference is traversed bottom-up. For each node $v$, a \emph{subtree MT order} $\text{SubtreeMT}(v)$ is computed as the average of $\text{NodeMT}$ over all aligned nodes in the subtree rooted at $v$. If no aligned nodes are present in the subtree, $\text{SubtreeMT}(v)$ is undefined. This value captures the preferred position of the subtree with respect to the hypothesis.

    \item \textbf{Reordering:}
    The tree is processed bottom-up. For each node $p$, we consider the ordered list consisting of $p$ and its immediate children.

    This list is scanned from left to right while maintaining a current position value. When visiting a node $v$, the value is updated as follows: if $v$ is the parent and $\text{NodeMT}(v)$ is defined, the value is set to $\text{NodeMT}(v)$; if $v$ is a child and $\text{SubtreeMT}(v)$ is defined, the value is set to $\text{SubtreeMT}(v)$; otherwise, the value is inherited from the preceding node. If the parent has no defined $\text{NodeMT}$, it also inherits the value from the preceding node.

    Each node is then assigned a sorting key equal to this value plus a small bias $\text{position}(v)/1000$, derived from its original position to ensure deterministic ordering and to mildly prefer the original word order.

    The nodes are sorted by this key, and the corresponding subtrees are reordered accordingly. Only immediate children are reordered at each step, and subtrees are always moved as whole units, preserving their internal structure. Sorting may leave the order unchanged if it already satisfies the desired ordering. Nodes without alignment inherit the value of their left neighbor, which biases the algorithm toward keeping nearby elements together when no reordering evidence is available.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Synthesis from a-layer to w-layer}
\label{sect:treex:a2w}
The word forms are already generated at the a-layer, so only minor post-processing is required. This step is performed using the TectoMT synthesis pipeline. Superfluous tokens are deleted (e.g. duplicated commas), prepositions are vocalized, the first letter in a sentence is capitalized, and the tokens are concatenated (a set of rules is used to decide which tokens should be space-delimited and which should not). The example in \Cref{reordering} results in the following sentence: \samppair{Internet vyvolal boom těchto spekulací}{The Internet has caused a boom of these speculations.}, which has the same meaning as the original reference sentence, is grammatically correct and, most importantly, is much more similar in its wording to the hypothesis.

\subsection{Results}
Our experiments are performed using datasets from the English-to-Czech translation task of the WMT12  \citep{wmt12} and WMT13 \citep{wmt13} competitions. For paraphrasing purposes, we employed two Czech paraphrase tables -- \fm (see \Cref{filtered_meteor}) and \wordnet \citep{czech-wordnet}, both of which contain lemmatized paraphrases.

Our method of paraphrasing is independent of the evaluation metric used. We employ three different metrics -- BLEU, \mn and \mn without paraphrase support. Using paraphrases in a metric on already paraphrased sentences seems redundant and furthermore, we showed that employing \mn without paraphrase support can be beneficial (as discussed in \cref{parmesan}). 

The results are presented in \Tref{results} as the Pearson correlation of each metric with human judgment. In both datasets, paraphrasing consistently improves correlation with human judgment. Notably, even the \mn metric, which includes paraphrase components, shows improved performance when utilizing references crafted from its own paraphrase tables. We hypothesize that this improvement is due to a reduction of noise within the paraphrase table, which otherwise blurs the difference between hypotheses from different MT systems.


Unlike the earlier substitution-based methods, the Treex-based approach modifies sentence structure only when supported by syntactic evidence, which limits both its gains and its error profile. The sentence reordering proves clearly beneficial when assessed using the BLEU metric, which penalizes any changes in word order to the reference sentence. Conversely, \mn is more forgiving of word order changes, and thus reordering has no significant impact on its scores.

In general, the rule-based paraphrasing approach operating at the deep syntactic layer demonstrates a consistent improvement in correlation with human judgment in datasets and evaluation metrics. Although the absolute gains are smaller than those achieved by the earlier simple substitution method, see \Cref{substitution}, the current approach offers a linguistically grounded alternative that enables controlled paraphrasing and explicit adjustment of word-order within a single unified framework.

We answered the following research questions:

\paragraph{Can targeted paraphrasing at the deep syntactic layer improve the correlation between automatic MT evaluation metrics and human judgment?} 
Yes. Experiments on WMT12 and WMT13 datasets show that paraphrasing reference sentences at the deep syntactic (t-)layer consistently improves the correlation of automatic metrics with human judgment. This holds across different evaluation metrics, namely BLEU and \mn.

\paragraph{How does a rule-based, linguistically motivated paraphrasing approach compare to our previous methods?}
In contrast to our previous SMT-based paraphrasing experiment, the rule-based Treex approach yields consistent improvements in correlation with human judgment. However, the earlier simple substitution-based methods achieve larger absolute gains in correlation. The main advantage of the Treex approach therefore lies not in maximizing correlation, but in providing greater linguistic control, avoiding metric-specific heuristics, and enabling syntactically constrained transformations, including word-order variation.

\paragraph{Does explicit word-order reordering of paraphrased references further improve MT evaluation?}
Yes, partially. Tree-based reordering at the analytical layer leads to additional improvements for BLEU, which in our experiments is sensitive to word order. In contrast, \mn, which is more tolerant to word-order variation, shows little or no additional benefit from reordering. These results suggest that the effect of reordering depends on the degree to which a given metric penalizes word-order differences.

\end{mdframed}

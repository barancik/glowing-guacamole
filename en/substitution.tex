\section{Simple substitution method}
\label{substitution}
%\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Simple substitution method}
In this section,\footnote{This Section is based on the article \citep{barancikova:2014}, a joint work with Rudolf Rosa and Aleš Tamchyna. Rudolf applied Depfix to all paraphrased sentences and Aleš conducted all experiments with alignments. My responsibilities included paraphrasing, filtering the noisy paraphrase tables, and evaluating the experiments.} we present a simple method for generating additional reference sentences by substituting lexical and phrasal paraphrases into existing references. Our approach is inspired by \citet{kauchak} but differs in three key aspects:
 
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Multiple substitutions in one sentence:} In the approach proposed by \cite{kauchak}, only one word per sentence is replaced. However, this is complicated in the Czech language. Czech is an~inflected language with rich morphology. A word typically has many forms and the correct form depends heavily on its context, e.g., the (morphological) case of a noun depends on the particular verb valency frame. Therefore, we allow multiple substitutions per sentence to produce one well-formed additional reference, rather than many partially broken ones.

\item \textbf{No context classifier:} Unlike previous work, we do not use a classifier to evaluate  contextual appropriateness. Instead, we focus on grammatical correctness and meaning preservation by applying Depfix \citep{depfix}, an automatic post-editing system capable of correcting grammatical errors in Czech sentences.

\item \textbf{Noisy sources of paraphrases:} While \citeauthor{kauchak} relies on the English WordNet as a high-quality source of paraphrases, the \wordnet{} \citep{czech-wordnet} is considerably smaller in scale. To overcome this, we utilize \cmt \citep{meteor-wmt:2011}, which provides much higher coverage but is inherently noisy due to its automatic construction via the pivot phrase method. 
To manage the noise, we adopt a tiered validation strategy: 
(i) for one-word substitutions, we restrict our source to \wordnet and \fm (\Cref{filtered_meteor}), a refined lemmatized subset of the original tables;
(ii) for multi-word substitutions, we use \cmpt to ensure sufficient coverage. 
To further mitigate the noise in multi-word substitutions, we experiment with constraining candidates to automatically aligned word or phrase pairs, as described in detail in \Cref{alignments}.
\end{enumerate}

 This section is organized as follows: \Cref{algorithm} details our four-stage paraphrasing pipeline, including candidate selection, validation against specific lexical and phrasal resources, various substitution strategies and postprocessing. While our methodology is metric-agnostic, we evaluate its effectiveness using the \bleu score in \Cref{substitution_results}, as it remains the most widely used metric in practice. Finally, supplementary experiments regarding the automatic filtering of noisy paraphrase tables are provided in Appendix~(see Section~\nameref{filtering-section}).

\subsection{Core Algorithm and Its Variants}
\label{algorithm}
We experiment with several variants of an algorithm for paraphrasing reference sentences. All variants consist of the following four steps:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(1)] \textbf{Candidate selection:} identifying potential pairs of words or phrases from a hypothesis and its corresponding reference, % (\Cref{candidates}), 
\item[(2)] \textbf{Validation:} verifying candidates against specific paraphrase tables (tiered approach: \fm for words, \cmpt for phrases), % (\Cref{paraphrasing}),
\item[(3)] \textbf{Paraphrasing:} applying validated candidates to the reference, % (\Cref{paraphrasing}),
\item[(4)] \textbf{Depfix:} applying automatic post-editing to resolve morphological or grammatical errors introduced during substitution. % (\Cref{depfix}).
\end{enumerate}

The algorithm variants differ primarily in three aspects: 
(i) how candidate paraphrase pairs are selected (Step 1), 
(ii) how validated candidates are applied to the reference (Step 3), and 
(iii) whether and how the Depfix post-editing step is applied (Step 4). 
The validation process (Step 2) remains consistent across all configurations.

\subsubsection{(1) Candidate Selection}
\label{candidates}
We use two different methods to select potential paraphrase pairs. The first is a~greedy search similar to the approach used by \citet{kauchak}. The second relies on automatic word alignment to select the corresponding segments of~a reference sentence and a hypothesis.

\paragraph{a. Simple Greedy Method}
\hfill \break
As the first step, we search for one-word paraphrase candidates. In this case, all sentences are used in their lemmatized forms. There are two reasons for this approach: (i) we want to avoid selecting different inflected forms of the same lemma as paraphrases, and (ii) one-word paraphrases in \wordnet and \fm are represented in the form of lemmas.

Let $ H_{L} $ and $ R_{L} $ be the sets of lemmas from a hypothesis and the corresponding reference sentence, respectively. The set of one-word paraphrase candidates $C_{L} $ is then defined as:

\begin{equation*}
C_{L} = \{(r,h) \, | \, r \in R_{L} \setminus H_{L} \wedge h \in H_{L} \setminus R_{L} \} 
\end{equation*}

Second, multi-word candidates $ C_M $ are selected as the Cartesian product of all contiguous word sequences in the reference sentence and in the hypothesis; in~contrast to $C_{L}$, $C_M$ is chosen directly from word forms, not lemmas. The maximum sequence length is set to seven words, which corresponds to the length of~the longest paraphrases in~\cmpt. Formally, let $ h_1,...,h_m $, $ r_1,..., r_n $  be the word forms in the  hypothesis and reference sentence, respectively. Then the set of multi-word paraphrase candidates is:

\begin{align*}
C_{M} = \{\, ( r_i \ldots r_{i+x} ,  h_j\ldots h_{j+y}) \mid &\
1 \leq i \leq n-x,\ 
1 \leq j \leq m-y,\\
& \ 0 \leq x,y \leq 6,\ 
(x \neq 0 \lor y \neq 0) \,\}.
\end{align*}

\paragraph{b. Word and Phrase Alignments}
\label{alignments}
\hfill \break
A potentially more reliable way to identify paraphrase candidates is to restrict substitutions to pairs of words or phrases that are aligned with each other. We compute word alignment between reference translations and the corresponding hypotheses using GIZA++ \citep{gizapp}, a widely adopted implementation of IBM Models and an HMM alignment model for word-level alignment in statistical machine translation. GIZA++ learns translation probabilities between tokens in two parallel corpora and induces word alignment links by iteratively improving a generative model of word correspondences.

Although GIZA++ typically performs best with large parallel corpora, the available data consisting solely of hypotheses paired with their corresponding reference translations are too small to yield high-quality alignments.\footnote{Hypotheses with corresponding reference sentences constitute only 75,039 sentence pairs (13 MT systems $\times$ 3003 sentences from WMT12 and 12 MT systems $\times$ 3000 sentences from WMT13). This dataset is insufficient for training a high-quality alignment.} To overcome this limitation, we take advantage of the fact that all system outputs translate the same source sentences. We therefore augment the training corpus with all possible pairs of system outputs, effectively treating every hypothesis as a \textit{translation} of every other. This results in over one million artificial Czech–Czech sentence pairs, greatly improving the statistical support available to GIZA++.\footnote{We also experiment with augmenting the data using additional Czech–Czech synthetic parallel corpora (generated with MT),  %\citep{czeng} prelozeny do cestiny 
but this does not improve paraphrasing quality. The simpler strategy with pairs of hypotheses therefore remains the preferred approach, as it requires no additional data.} For example, the parallel data for WMT12 then looks as follows:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
Source & Target \\
\hline
system 1 & reference \\
system 1 & system 2 \\
system 1 & system 3 \\
... & ...\\
system 1 & system 13 \\
system 2 & reference \\
system 2 & system 1 \\
system 2 & system 3 \\
... & ... \\
system 13 & system 12 \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}


GIZA++ is trained bidirectionally (reference~$\rightarrow$~hypothesis and hypothesis~$\rightarrow$~reference), and the resulting alignments are symmetrized. The set of one-word paraphrase candidates  $C_{L}$ is then defined as all word pairs (more precisely, their lemmata) connected by at least one alignment link. Multi-word candidates  $C_{M}$ are extracted using standard phrase extraction procedures for phrase-based MT, applying the usual phrase consistency criterion \citep{Och99improvedalignment}. This approach ensures that multi-word paraphrases represent phrases that are consistently aligned as units, filtering out many unrealistic or noisy combinations that would otherwise arise from a purely greedy selection.

\subsubsection{(2) Validation}
\label{sec:validation}
Once the candidate sets $C_{L}$ and $C_{M}$ are extracted, we validate them to ensure semantic equivalence. To balance coverage and precision,  we use a tiered validation strategy based on the length of the candidate:
\begin{itemize}
	\item for single-word candidates ($C_{L}$): We use \wordnet and \fm{}. These sources provide high-quality lexical equivalents. If a lemma appears in multiple candidate pairs we give preference to those found in~\wordnet or, even better, in the intersection of both paraphrase tables, as these are more likely to represent reliable lexical paraphrases.
	\item  for multi-word candidates ($C_{M}$): We utilize the \cmpt{}. Because multi-word variations are rarely covered by WordNet, we rely on these larger, albeit noisier, automatically generated tables.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{(3) Paraphrasing}
\label{paraphrasing}
We evaluate three paraphrasing strategies, which differ in the order in which one-word and multi-word substitutions are applied.

\begin{description}
\item[One-word only:] 
We proceed word by word from the beginning of the reference sentence to~its end. If the~lemma of~the current reference word appears as~the first member of~a~paraphrase pair from $ C_{L} $, it is replaced by~the corresponding word of the hypothesis (i.e., the second element of~that pair). Otherwise, we keep the original word from the reference sentence. This variant uses only one-word substitutions and does not consider any multi-word paraphrases.

\item[One-word first] 
We begin by applying the \textit{One-word only} method. After all one-word substitutions, we apply multi-word paraphrases. Multi-word substitutions are used by applying the longest possibility first and then decreasing the length of the phrase. This ordering is important because \cmpt frequently contains both multi-word paraphrases and their subsequences; applying shorter paraphrases first could block a valid longer substitution. We do not replace any word that was already changed by the \textit{One-word only} method. 

\item[Multi-word first:] 
We begin by applying multi-word substitutions, again proceeding from the longest to the shortest phrase pairs in $C_{M}$. Only sequences that have not yet been modified are eligible for replacement. After all possible multi-word substitutions have been applied, we perform a second pass using the \textit{One-word only} method to substitute any remaining unmatched words.
\end{description}


\subsubsection{(4) Depfix}
\label{depfix}
Depfix \citep{depfix} is an automatic post-editing system originally developed to improve English-to-Czech phrase-based machine translation. It consists of a~set of~linguistically-motivated rules combined with a~statistical component, designed to~correct various types of errors, particularly those related to grammar. Using a range of natural language processing tools, Depfix analyzes the input sentences and attempts to fix common translation artifacts. For the purposes of this work, we adapt Depfix to suit the specific characteristics of our paraphrasing setting.

During preliminary analysis, we observe a similarity between errors in the outputs of our paraphrasing algorithms and those commonly encountered in phrase-based machine translation systems. In particular, errors in morphological agreement occur frequently. This observation suggests that Depfix might also be well suited for correcting substitution-related grammatical issues. Unlike conventional grammar-checking tools found in word processors, Depfix targets error types characteristic of machine-generated text rather than errors typical of human writing.

Furthermore, Depfix benefits from access to the English source sentence, which it uses to guide some of its corrections. This provides an advantage in our scenario: while traditional grammar correction tools typically lack access to the source sentence, Depfix uses the source context to improve the accuracy of its modifications. For these reasons, we apply Depfix as a post-editing step to reduce grammatical errors in our paraphrased outputs.

However, it is worth noting that certain error types, commonly encountered in phrase-based machine translation, such as inaccuracies in preserving the correct verb tense, do not frequently appear in the paraphrasing process and may even introduce errors. To account for this, we evaluate two configurations of Depfix:

\begin{description}
\item[full:] The original Depfix system with all 33 fixing blocks, as~described in \cite{rosa:mgr}.
\item[limited:] A manually guided analysis of 50 randomly selected outputs reveals that while Depfix often corrects genuine errors, it may also introduce new ones. Most of these errors correspond to changes crucial when translating from English (e.g., adding missing negation) but redundant for paraphrasing. To address this, Depfix is adapted for fixing paraphrasing errors by disabling 10 fixing blocks\footnote{The following fixing blocks are disabled in limited Depfix: Fixing reflexive tantum, Fixing morphological number of nouns, Translation of \equo{by}, Translation of \equo{of}, Translation of present continuous, Subject categories projection, Missing reflexive verbs, Subject personal pronouns dropping, Tense translation, Negation translation}, identified as the main sources of incorrect modifications.
\end{description}

On the other hand, applying Depfix is consistently beneficial, supporting our assumption that the grammatical correctness of reference sentences plays an important role in reliable MT evaluation. The differences between the \emph{full} and \emph{limited} Depfix configurations are small and not statistically significant; therefore, we do not claim a consistent advantage of either variant.

Results on the WMT12 and WMT13 data are consistent. Paraphrasing increases the accuracy of the evaluation using the \bleu metric, although the differences in~the WMT13 data are not as~prominent due to a much higher baseline. This is also reflected in~the smaller amount of substitutions (see \Cref{substitutions:12:13}).

Furthermore, we experiment with automatic filtering of \fm and \cmpt using \emph{One-word only} and \emph{Multi-word first}, respectively. The results are inconclusive and we present them in more detail in Section \nameref{filtering-section} of Appendix.

\subsection{Conclusion}
Our findings confirm the positive impact of paraphrasing reference sentences on the performance of the \bleu metric. Among the examined strategies, the \emph{One-word only} greedy substitution method achieves the best results. We also show that Depfix is beneficial in ensuring grammatical correctness of paraphrased references. Finally, we explore several techniques for reducing noise in \mpt. 

\begin{mdframed}[
    linecolor=gray,
    linewidth=2pt,
    roundcorner=5,
    backgroundcolor=gray!5
]
\noindent
We answer the following research questions:

\paragraph{Does paraphrase substitution improve the correlation of \bleu with human judgments?}
Yes. Using the additional references produced by \emph{One-word only} plus Depfix increases system-level Pearson correlation from 0.75 to 0.83 on WMT12 and from 0.84 to 0.89 on WMT13 (\Cref{corrs:12:13}). The improvement over the baseline is statistically significant.

\paragraph{Are multi-word paraphrases beneficial?}
Generally no. Multi-word substitutions are noisy: while they can raise raw \bleu by pulling hypotheses closer lexically, they frequently introduce non-equivalences or grammatical issues and blur system differences. Even with filtering, \emph{Multi-word first} does not outperform \emph{One-word only} or \emph{One-word first} on either WMT12 or WMT13.

\paragraph{Does constraining substitutions to aligned word/phrase pairs help?}
No. Alignment constraints reduce the number of applicable substitutions too aggressively and act as a bottleneck, preventing a sufficient volume of lexical variations from being generated, which limits the improvement in \bleu correlation.

\paragraph{Does post-editing with Depfix help, and which configuration is better?}
Yes. Depfix consistently improves correlation, confirming the importance of the grammatical well-formedness of the generated references. The \emph{full} and \emph{limited} configurations perform similarly, with no statistically significant differences, suggesting that the standard Depfix configuration is sufficiently robust for this task.

\paragraph{Can we automatically filter noisy paraphrase tables without hurting performance?}
Partly. Lexical pivoting scores allow heavy pruning (discarding up to $\sim$80\%) while retaining high correlation for one-word substitutions, but they do not surpass the best unfiltered \emph{One-word only} result. Pivot scores from \mpt perform worse than random scores in our evaluation. 


\end{mdframed}
